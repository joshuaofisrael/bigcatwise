Why do leopards drag their kills into trees? To beat the hyenas

By the BigCatWise team | Published 12 October 2026 | Last updated 12 October 2026 | Last reviewed 12 October 2026

Leopards drag their kills into trees mainly to stop other predators stealing them, especially spotted hyenas. A study of 2,032 kills made by 104 leopards in South Africa's Sabi Sand Game Reserve found that leopards hoisted 51% of their kills, that at least 21% of kills were stolen, mostly by hyenas, and that kills left on the ground were more likely to be lost. Females that lost more kills raised fewer cubs.

The short answer

Hoisting a carcass into a tree is a form of food caching: the leopard stores its meal somewhere other animals cannot easily reach and comes back to feed over time. Researchers have proposed four possible reasons for it, which can overlap. The evidence from the largest study so far points clearly to one of them.

Avoiding theft (kleptoparasitism)Keep the kill away from hyenas and lionsStrongly supported

The study: 2,032 kills by 104 leopards

Guy Balme, Jennifer Miller, Ross Pitman and Luke Hunter studied leopards in the Sabi Sand Game Reserve, next to Kruger National Park in South Africa, and published the results in the Journal of Animal Ecology in 2017. They used records of 2,032 prey items killed by 104 known leopards between 2013 and 2015. For each kill they looked at whether it was hoisted, how long the leopard fed on it and whether another animal took it, then tested how this varied with the leopard's sex and age, the size of the prey, vegetation, elevation, weather, and the risk posed by dominant competitors such as hyenas and lions.

Leopards hoisted 51% of their kills. At least 21% of all kills were kleptoparasitized, the scientific term for having food stolen by another animal, and most of the thieves were spotted hyenas (Crocuta crocuta).

Which kills get hoisted?

Leopards did not hoist everything. They were most likely to haul up kills of an intermediate size. A very small prey animal can be eaten quickly on the spot, so there is little to protect, while a very large one may be too heavy to drag up a trunk. Leopards were also more likely to hoist when certain competitors were around, and less likely to hoist during a resource pulse, a period when prey was abundant and easy to catch. That last result counts against the idea that tree caching is mainly about storing surplus food.

Once a kill was in a tree, the leopard fed on it for longer than on kills left on the ground. In other words, the tree acts as a safe larder that lets the cat return to the same carcass.

Which kills get stolen?

Kills were more likely to be stolen when the prey was larger, when it was not hoisted, and in parts of the reserve where the chance of meeting hyenas was highest. Thefts were also more likely at lower temperatures. Spotted hyenas are poor climbers, so a carcass wedged in the fork of a tree is much harder for them to reach than one lying in the grass. Why it matters: lost kills mean fewer cubs

The most striking result is about breeding. Female leopards that lost a higher share of their kills to thieves had lower annual reproductive success than females that lost fewer kills. Stolen meat is not just a missed meal; for a mother raising cubs alone it can mean fewer surviving young. The authors concluded that their results strongly support the kleptoparasitism avoidance explanation, and that hoisting is a key adaptation that lets leopards live alongside high densities of larger competitors.

Does this shape what leopards hunt?

Possibly. A separate review by Matt Hayward and colleagues, published in the Journal of Zoology in 2006, analysed 33 studies of leopard diet from 13 countries. It found that leopards preferentially prey on species weighing roughly 10 to 40 kg, with a most preferred prey mass of about 25 kg. Impala, bushbuck and common duiker were significantly preferred. The authors noted that leopards favour prey that lives in small herds and dense habitat and poses little risk of injury during capture.

The 2017 study adds a twist. Its authors argued that leopards may pick smaller prey than simple optimal foraging theory would predict, trading the extra meat from a big kill against the higher chance of losing it to hyenas. A 25 kg impala that fits in a tree may be worth more to a leopard than a bigger animal it cannot protect.

Do other big cats do this?

Tree caching is a leopard speciality. Lions and tigers are much heavier, live in different settings and handle competitors differently: lions defend kills as a group, which is one of the themes in why do lions live in prides? Cheetahs, by contrast, often lose kills to lions and hyenas and eat quickly instead. You can compare how the spotted cats differ in leopard vs jaguar vs cheetah.

Key takeaways

  • In Sabi Sand, leopards hoisted 51% of 2,032 recorded kills.
  • At least 21% of kills were stolen, mostly by spotted hyenas, and kills left on the ground were more likely to be taken.
  • Leopards hoisted mid sized kills most often and fed longer on hoisted kills.
  • Females that lost more kills had lower annual reproductive success.
  • Leopards prefer prey of about 10 to 40 kg, a size they can kill safely and often carry.

Why do leopards drag their kills into trees?

Mainly to stop other predators stealing them. A study of 2,032 kills in South Africa found that kills left on the ground were more likely to be stolen, mostly by spotted hyenas, and the authors concluded that hoisting is chiefly a way to avoid theft.
